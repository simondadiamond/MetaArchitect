Opportunity Report

The monthly missing-document chase, in a six-person bookkeeping practice.

This is what the C$350 session produces. The workflow below is real and common; the practice in it is composed, and no client data appears anywhere in this document.

01

Current workflow

Between the 1st and the 12th of each month, a bookkeeper works a spreadsheet of forty-one clients and chases whatever is missing: a bank statement, a credit card export, a stack of receipts, an answer about one transaction nobody recognises.

The chase is email, then a text message, then a phone call. Each reply arrives in a different form — a photo, a PDF, a forwarded statement, a sentence in the body of an email — and is filed by hand into the client folder. The spreadsheet is updated from memory at the end of the day, or it is not.

Nothing starts until the documents are in. Month-end close for the whole practice waits on the slowest fifteen percent of clients.

02

Friction

  • The state of the chase lives in one person’s head. When she is off, nobody else can tell who has been asked twice and who has not been asked at all.
  • Every follow-up is composed from scratch. Same four sentences, forty-one times, in two languages.
  • Arrival is invisible. A document that lands on the 3rd may not be noticed until the 9th, because noticing requires opening the inbox and remembering what was outstanding.
  • Filing is manual and inconsistent. Two people name the same document three ways.
  • The owner is the escalation path. Anything awkward — a client who has not replied twice, a document that looks wrong — becomes a decision only she makes, at 9pm.

Estimated at eleven to fourteen hours per month across the practice, concentrated in the eight working days when there is least room for it.

03

Proposed workflow

A single checklist per client per period becomes the system of record, generated from the client list rather than maintained by hand. Everything else hangs off it.

  • On the 1st, outstanding items are derived from what the practice already holds, and a first request goes out per client in their own language.
  • Inbound replies are read for attachments, matched to the client and the requested item, named to the practice’s convention and filed.
  • A reply that cannot be matched with confidence is not guessed at. It is queued for a person, with the reason it could not be matched.
  • Reminders escalate on a schedule the practice sets — day 4 gentle, day 8 firmer, day 11 flagged to the owner — and stop the moment the item arrives.
  • One status view shows every client, every outstanding item and the age of each request.

Acceptance criteria, as they would be written

  • CoverageOver one full period, every client on the list receives a first request on the 1st and no client is missed.
  • MatchingOf documents that arrive by email, at least 90% are filed to the right client and the right item without a person touching them. The remainder are queued, not misfiled — a wrong file counts as a failure, an escalation does not.
  • RemindersNo reminder is sent for an item already received. This is the one that gets a practice complained about, so it is a hard failure, not a percentage.
  • TimeHands-on time for the chase, measured over the period, is under four hours across the practice.
  • RecoverabilityIf the workflow is switched off mid-period, the practice can finish the month by hand from what is already filed, with no reconstruction.

These are drafted in the session and agreed in writing before implementation. They are what the fourteen-day live run is validated against, and what the final payment is due on.

04

What stays human

  • Any document the matcher is not confident about.
  • Every escalation past the second reminder, and every message to a client who has gone quiet.
  • Anything that looks wrong rather than missing — a statement with a gap in it, a total that does not agree.
  • The decision to close a period.

The point is not that a person checks the work. The point is that the workflow knows which cases it is not entitled to decide, and stops.

What it does when it is unsure

  • Two clients could matchFiles nothing. Queues the message with both candidates named and the reason for the ambiguity.
  • The document is the right client, wrong periodFiles it, marks the item still outstanding, and says so in the queue. Filing it is safe; closing the item is not.
  • A statement is short pages, or the totals do not agreeEscalates. This is a “looks wrong” case, and the workflow has no business forming an opinion about it.
  • The client replies with a question instead of a documentStops the reminder clock and escalates. Nothing is worse than a chase that keeps chasing someone who already answered.
  • The mailbox or the document store is unreachableSends nothing, retries, and tells a person after the second failure. It never sends a request it cannot record.

Every one of these was chosen because getting it wrong costs the practice a client relationship rather than an hour. That is the line the approval step is drawn on.

05

What gets automated

  • Building the outstanding-items list at the start of each period.
  • Sending the first request and the scheduled reminders, in the client’s language.
  • Reading inbound mail for attachments and classifying them.
  • Naming and filing to the practice’s existing folder convention.
  • Marking items received and stopping their reminders.
  • The status view, and a Monday summary of what is still outstanding and how old it is.

06

Systems involved

  • The practice’s existing mailbox, via its own API rather than a forwarding rule.
  • The existing document store, with the existing folder structure unchanged.
  • The client list already maintained in the accounting package.
  • A hosted language model for classification and for drafting the reminders, with a documented boundary on what is sent to it.
  • One small scheduled service, hosted on the practice’s own account.

No practice-management platform is introduced. If the practice adopts one later, the workflow is retired rather than migrated — that is the honest trade and it is stated up front.

07

Expected impact

  • The eleven to fourteen hours becomes an estimated two to three, spent on the exceptions rather than on the chase.
  • Documents are noticed on the day they arrive rather than on the day someone looks.
  • The state of every client is visible to anyone in the practice, not to one person.
  • The owner sees the escalations and nothing else.

These are estimates made from the practice’s own numbers during the session, not benchmarks. The measurement that matters is the one taken during the live run.

How the number actually gets taken

  • BaselineOne period before anything is built. The bookkeeper notes start and stop times against the chase, on paper, five times a week. Crude, and it is the only honest baseline available, because nothing currently records this.
  • AfterThe same tally over the fourteen-day live run, plus the queue’s own count of how many items needed a person.
  • The number that decidesHands-on hours for the period, compared against the acceptance criterion of four. Not documents processed, not messages sent — those go up, and they are not the point.
  • What would invalidate itAn unusually light month, or a period run while someone was away. If the live run lands on one of those, it runs again rather than being written up.

08

Implementation options

  • Do nothing. The chase is survivable and it has been survived. Worth naming, because the cost is a known quantity and the alternative is not free.
  • Do the cheap half. Templated requests and a shared checklist, no classification and no filing. Perhaps a third of the benefit for a small fraction of the work, and it can be built in-house.
  • Buy a practice-management platform. TaxDome and Karbon both do document chasing. If the practice wants the rest of what they do, this is the better purchase and I will say so.
  • Build the workflow above. One Sprint. Appropriate if the practice does not want the platform, or wants this one job solved properly first.

A recommendation is only a recommendation if it can come out the other way. In this composed example the second option is the honest starting point, and the fourth is worth it only if the practice intends to keep growing on its current stack.
